Why each hue

CoreNavy ground with the reference bird's teal. The neutral base every other profile sits on; it claims no domain.
MathematicsBurgundy: a deep, low-lightness red, apart from the alarm reds used for errors. Reads as ink and binding, for derivation and proof.
ScienceSky blue, the lightest chromatic plate: open field, observation. Blue stays distinct under red-green colour-vision deficiency, which shifts reds and greens and leaves the blue-yellow axis.
EngineeringOrange, the hue safety signage uses for warning (ANSI Z535 pairs orange with its WARNING signal word). Fits work where a failure has consequences.
CryptographyDeep violet: the darkest chromatic plate, at the far end of the spectrum from orange. Concealment and keys.
StatisticsWhite plate, ink-blue marks: the empty plot area. Deliberately achromatic so that data colours placed on it are not biased by a tint.
Quality AssuranceLime green: the conventional pass hue, shifted toward yellow so it stays apart from the Commercial teal and survives red-green deficiency by lightness.

What the measurements say, including what they do not support

Every text pairing above is at or over 4.5 to 1 (WCAG 2.2, 1.4.3). Edition fields stay at least 27.5 units apart in OKLab under every simulated colour-vision deficiency.

Hue alone does not separate every pair of modules for every viewer. The closest pair of workspace accents is cryptography and statistics (commercial field, deuteranopia): 0.8 units, where about 2 is just noticeable. Module plates are closest for core and cryptography under deuteranopia (3.0 units). This is why each module also has a name and a wing mark, and why no colour is ever the only code (WCAG 1.4.1). These are computed figures. Deficiency is simulated; no test with people, print proof or accessibility audit was performed.

Colour decides nothing in a review. A workspace's colour repeats what its name already says.
